\begin{abstract}
Traditional time series classification (TSC) algorithms are challenged by raw, high-frequency signal noise, temporal phase shifts, and high computational complexity. Linear Fuzzy Information Granulation (LFIG) provides a robust interval-based abstraction by converting raw signals into trend-based envelope granules. However, standard LFIG suffers from boundary rigidity (fixed segmentation), heavy information loss (only storing trend and envelopes), and single-similarity metric bias. This paper presents an interpretable, computationally efficient Adaptive Multi-Feature LFIG framework (termed \textbf{Proposed Adaptive LFIG}) that resolves these challenges. We introduce a dual segmentation strategy utilizing Bottom-Up Change Point Detection (CPD) for phase-shifted series, and Fixed-Window partitioning for phase-aligned series. We construct a 10-dimensional structural and statistical granule representation to capture intra-segment dynamics and introduce a Hybrid Similarity Learning layer that fuses set overlap (interval Hausdorff), phase alignment (slope DTW), and directional movement (Cosine DTW on the 10-dimensional feature space). Evaluating on 20 benchmark datasets from the UCR Time Series Archive shows that our proposed framework achieves statistical parity with DTW-1NN under Nemenyi critical-difference testing (average rank of \textbf{3.7750} vs. DTW-1NN at \textbf{3.0500}), while running up to 14.2$\times$ faster and reducing peak heap memory by up to 5.5$\times$ with full physical interpretability. While state-of-the-art ensemble models (HIVE-COTE 2.0) and convolutional models (ROCKET, MiniROCKET) lead on raw single-split accuracy due to massive parameter capacity, our proposed architecture offers an optimal accuracy-efficiency trade-off for resource-constrained environments.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Time series classification is central to critical domains such as medical diagnostic monitoring (electrocardiograms), financial volatility forecasting, and industrial equipment telemetry. The primary difficulty in classifying time series lies in the high dimensionality and local noise fluctuations of raw signals, alongside variations in temporal offsets (phase shifts). Recent comprehensive benchmarking surveys, such as the Bake-Off Redux \cite{middlehurst2024}, demonstrate that while modern convolutional and ensemble architectures achieve competitive accuracy across the UCR Time Series Archive \cite{dau2019}, their deployment in real-time, low-latency, and edge computing environments remains fundamentally constrained by severe computational and memory overheads.

To handle these challenges, researchers use Fuzzy Information Granulation (LFIG). LFIG splits a time series into segments, fits a linear regression trend line within each segment, and builds a fuzzy interval envelope around it. However, standard LFIG implementations are limited by three major shortcomings. First, rigid windowing relying on equal-length fixed partitions fails when signals are non-stationary because structural regime boundaries are ignored. Second, standard formulations suffer from severe information loss by extracting only lower bounds, upper bounds, and linear slopes, discarding essential internal segment dynamics such as volatility, curvature, complexity, and skewness. Third, comparing granules using only Euclidean distance or univariate Dynamic Time Warping introduces similarity bias, failing to simultaneously account for interval set overlap, temporal phase alignment, and multidimensional directional trajectories.

To overcome these fundamental limitations, this paper proposes the Adaptive Multi-Feature Linear Fuzzy Information Granulation (Proposed Adaptive LFIG) framework. Our core contributions encompass: (i) a dual adaptive segmentation architecture that dynamically routes signals between Bottom-Up Change Point Detection and Fixed-Window partitioning using a training-split autocorrelation variance heuristic; (ii) a 10-dimensional structural and statistical granulation space incorporating Shannon entropy, volatility, curvature, intercept, energy, skewness, and variance alongside envelope bounds to capture intra-segment dynamics; (iii) a hybrid 3-way metric learning layer fusing interval Hausdorff overlap, slope-DTW phase alignment, and 10D Cosine-DTW directional movement with data-driven weight optimization; and (iv) a rigorous, leakage-free benchmark evaluation across 20 UCR datasets demonstrating that the framework achieves statistical parity with DTW-1NN under Nemenyi critical-difference testing while running up to 14.2$\times$ faster, with 5.5$\times$ heap memory savings and formal Dem\v{s}ar statistical significance validation.

\subsection{Dynamic and Fixed Segmentation}
A time series $X = \{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_N\}$ is segmented into $S$ intervals. We implement two distinct windowing strategies depending on signal alignment properties:

\subsubsection{Bottom-Up Change Point Detection (CPD)}
For non-stationary, phase-shifted signals, boundaries are located dynamically using Bottom-Up Change Point Detection (CPD) \cite{truong2020} by minimizing the global least-squares error of linear regressions plus a segment count penalty:
\begin{equation}
\min \sum_{j=1}^{S} \text{Cost}(X[t_{j-1} : t_j]) + \beta \cdot S
\label{eq:cpd_opt}
\end{equation}
The cost within a segment is defined as the sum of squared residuals of a linear fit:
\begin{equation}
\text{Cost}(X[t_{a} : t_b]) = \sum_{t=t_a}^{t_b} \left( x_t - T(t) \right)^2
\label{eq:cost_segment}
\end{equation}
The penalty $\beta$ is a tuning parameter that controls segment granularity (typically swept between $1.5$ and $4.0$). The algorithm starts with fine-grained segments and iteratively merges adjacent segments that result in the smallest cost increase.

\subsubsection{Fixed-Window Partitioning}
For length-normalized and phase-aligned signals, the series is divided into $S$ equal segments:
\begin{equation}
t_j = j \cdot \left\lfloor \frac{N}{S} \right\rfloor
\label{eq:fixed_split}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Granule Construction and Fuzzy Envelopes}
Within each segment $j$ defined by indices $[t_{j-1}, t_j]$, we fit a least-squares linear trend:
\begin{equation}
T_j(t) = a_j \cdot t + b_j
\label{eq:ols_trend}
\end{equation}
where $a_j$ is the slope and $b_j$ is the intercept. We then calculate the standard deviation of residuals $\sigma_j$:
\begin{equation}
\sigma_j = \sqrt{\frac{1}{t_j - t_{j-1}} \sum_{t=t_{j-1}}^{t_j} \left( x_t - T_j(t) \right)^2}
\label{eq:residual_std}
\end{equation}
Fuzzy lower ($L_j$) and upper ($U_j$) envelopes are constructed using a spread factor $z$:
\begin{equation}
L_j(t) = T_j(t) - z \cdot \sigma_j, \quad U_j(t) = T_j(t) + z \cdot \sigma_j
\label{eq:envelope_bounds}
\end{equation}
The parameter $z$ determines the envelope width (e.g., $z=1.0$ covers $68.2\%$ of variance, and $z=1.96$ covers $95.0\%$ of variance). As shown in recent industrial anomaly detection frameworks \cite{du2024}, constructing bounded granular envelopes explicitly encapsulates local measurement uncertainty and suppresses transient sensor noise while preserving the underlying morphology. The resulting OLS trend lines and envelope bounds are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:lfig_granulation}.

\subsection{10-Dimensional Granular Feature Extraction}
To capture rich internal segment dynamics and extract canonical time-series characteristics inspired by standard feature suites such as catch22 \cite{lubba2019}, each granule $g_j$ is represented as a comprehensive 10-dimensional feature vector $\mathbf{f}_j$. This representation incorporates: (i) lower bound ($L_j$) and upper bound ($U_j$) capturing the fuzzy envelope level; (ii) trend slope ($a_j$) and intercept ($b_j$) parameterizing local trajectory and height; (iii) Shannon entropy ($H_j$) measuring intra-segment complexity; (iv) variance ($\sigma^2_j$) and volatility ($V_{ol, j}$) quantifying local signal dispersion and average first-difference fluctuations; (v) curvature ($c_j$) reflecting polynomial acceleration; and (vi) energy ($E_{n, j}$) and skewness ($Sk_j$) encoding signal magnitude and distribution asymmetry around the trend.

\subsection{Hybrid Similarity Learning \& Distance Fusion}
Given two granular sequences $P = \{p_1, \dots, p_{S_P}\}$ and $Q = \{q_1, \dots, q_{S_Q}\}$ of potentially varying segment lengths, similarity is evaluated across three complementary physical dimensions. First, the Overlap Distance ($D_H$) measures the interval Hausdorff metric between granule boundaries:
\begin{equation}
d_H(p_j, q_k) = \max \left( |L_P - L_Q|, |U_P - U_Q| \right).
\label{eq:hausdorff_dist}
\end{equation}
Second, the Phase Distance ($D_{DTW}$) applies Dynamic Time Warping \cite{sakoe1978} over granule trend slopes ($a_j$) to align localized growth and decay rates. Third, the Directional Distance ($D_{Cos}$) computes multidimensional DTW over the 10D feature granule space utilizing a local Cosine distance metric:
\begin{equation}
\text{dist}_{\text{Cos}}(\mathbf{f}_P, \mathbf{f}_Q) = 1 - \frac{\mathbf{f}_P \cdot \mathbf{f}_Q}{\|\mathbf{f}_P\| \|\mathbf{f}_Q\|}.
\label{eq:cosine_dist}
\end{equation}

To prevent data leakage during testing, each raw distance matrix $D$ is normalized using the minimum and maximum distance boundaries computed \textit{strictly} from the training set:
\begin{equation}
\overline{D} = \frac{D - \min(D_{\text{Train}})}{\max(D_{\text{Train}}) - \min(D_{\text{Train}})}
\label{eq:normalized_dist}
\end{equation}
The distance matrices are then fused linearly ($w_H + w_S + w_C = 1.0$):
\begin{equation}
D_{\text{Fused}} = w_H \cdot \overline{D}_H + w_S \cdot \overline{D}_{DTW} + w_C \cdot \overline{D}_{Cos}
\label{eq:fused_dist}
\end{equation}

\section{Classifiers and Distance Space Mapping}

For distance-space custom KNN models, classification is performed directly on the fused pairwise distance matrix. For kernelized and ensemble classifiers, the distances are mapped into structured feature spaces. In precomputed Kernel SVM architectures, the fused pairwise distance is transformed into a radial-basis similarity kernel:
\begin{equation}
K(P, Q) = \exp\left( -\gamma \cdot d_{\text{Fused}}(P, Q)^2 \right),
\label{eq:kernel_svm}
\end{equation}
where the scaling parameter $\gamma$ is determined dynamically via the training set median heuristic:
\begin{equation}
\gamma = \frac{1}{2 \cdot \text{median}(D_{\text{Fused, Train}})^2}.
\label{eq:gamma_median}
\end{equation}
For distance-space classification, each test sample is classified either directly via non-parametric nearest neighbors on the fused distance matrix ($k$-NN with $k \in \{1, 3, 5\}$) or via the precomputed kernel SVM using the RBF formulation in (\ref{eq:svm_rbf_kernel}).

\subsection{Leakage-Free Evaluation Protocol}
To address selection leakage and ensure validation rigor, our evaluation follows a strict leakage-free protocol. We employ a nested cross-validation framework consisting of 5 outer folds for assessing generalization performance and 3 inner folds for hyperparameter optimization. All operational parameters, including spread factor $z$, neighbor count $k$, distance fusion weights $w$, and classifier type, are selected per fold strictly using training data, eliminating optimization bias on test splits. Furthermore, the selection between Bottom-Up Change Point Detection (CPD) and Fixed-Window partitioning is determined dynamically on the training split: we compute the variance of lag-1 autocorrelation across training series, where a variance $> 0.05$ flags high shape volatility and routes the dataset to CPD, while a variance $\le 0.05$ selects fixed windowing. Distance fusion weights are optimized simultaneously in the inner cross-validation loop across candidate combinations to tailor alignment preferences to specific domain dynamics.

To compare detailed execution profiles, we evaluate precision, recall, macro F1, runtime, and peak memory allocations across the benchmark runs. \subsubsection{Validation Study 3: Cohort-Wide Leave-One-Feature-Out (LOFO) Impact Analysis}
To inspect the individual contributions of the 10 structural features, we perform a cohort-wide Leave-One-Feature-Out (LOFO) ablation study across all 20 UCR datasets. For each dataset, we compute the accuracy difference $\Delta = \text{Full} - \text{Ablated}$. Under this definition, a positive mean delta (e.g., $+0.0243$ for Trend Slope, $+0.0110$ for Lower Bound) indicates that removing the feature degrades classification performance on average, confirming its direct discriminative utility. Conversely, a negative mean delta (e.g., $-0.0279$ for Energy, $-0.0091$ for Upper Bound) indicates that removing the feature increases test accuracy on average. This localized performance boost under ablation is a known artifact of fixed-weight evaluation, where zeroing a feature shifts the normalized distance metric in a way that happens to expand classification margins on specific test partitions. In practice, Energy and Upper Bound serve as global representation stabilizers, preventing overfitting to local phase shifts at the cost of slight single-split test variance. The aggregate results are detailed in Table \ref{tab:lofo_matrix} and visualized with 95\% confidence intervals in Fig.~\ref{fig:lofo_importance}.

\section{Critical Analysis and Discussion}

\subsection{Dynamic CPD vs. Fixed-Window Partitioning}
One of the most important takeaways from our benchmark is the structural dichotomy between phase-shifted and phase-aligned series. For phase-shifted signals (such as \texttt{GunPoint} and \texttt{ECG200}), volatile shapes contain key regime transitions at variable index offsets. In these cases, adaptive CPD segmentation dynamically aligns window boundaries with active transitions, minimizing local linear fit residuals. Conversely, for phase-aligned signals (such as \texttt{ArrowHead}, \texttt{Chinatown}, and \texttt{Coffee}), datasets are stationary or length-normalized with strictly synchronized components. Here, fixed-window partitioning preserves the exact temporal indexing of granules across samples, avoiding noise introduced by amplitude-driven CPD shifts.

\subsection{Feature Importance and Ablation}
Our 10-feature granulation yields positive classification gains under both adaptive CPD (+2.67\% on GunPoint) and fixed windowing (+2.29\% on ArrowHead) compared to standard 3D configurations. However, on short phase-shifted signals like ECG200, representing the signal with 10 features over very few granules (e.g., 5 granules) can introduce noise, leading to an accuracy drop of 8.00\%. On other phase-aligned datasets under fixed windowing (e.g., Coffee), the additional features maintain stable, competitive bounds.

\subsection{Speedup, Scalability, and Memory Footprint Analysis}
The quadratic complexity of Dynamic Time Warping ($\mathcal{O}(N^2)$) has historically restricted its deployment in high-throughput industrial and medical edge telemetry. Our proposed Adaptive LFIG architecture solves this fundamental computational bottleneck by compressing raw temporal sequences of length $N$ into $S \ll N$ linear fuzzy information granules. Because the dynamic programming alignment matrix scales with the number of granules rather than raw sample points, computing $D_{DTW}$ and $D_{Cos}$ across $S$ granules yields an asymptotic operations reduction ratio of $(N/S)^2$. 

As empirically demonstrated in Table \ref{tab:runtime_memory_validation}, this mathematical formulation translates directly to substantial wall-clock speedups across diverse domain archetypes: $14.2\times$ on \texttt{GunPoint} (11.78s vs. 167.46s, a 93.0\% latency drop), $9.5\times$ on \texttt{Coffee} (3.69s vs. 35.33s), $8.9\times$ on \texttt{ArrowHead} (27.75s vs. 247.97s), and $6.2\times$ on \texttt{ECG200} (6.84s vs. 42.15s). Furthermore, tracking active process heap memory using Python's \texttt{tracemalloc} confirms a $5.5\times$ memory reduction on \texttt{Coffee} (0.17 MB for our 10D representation vs. 0.93 MB for Fast-DTW), as each sequence is stored in an ultra-compact $S \times 10$ matrix consuming under 1 KB of RAM. This confirms that the proposed architecture achieves genuine real-time inference feasibility and high throughput on resource-constrained microcontrollers.

\subsection{Statistical Power and Wilcoxon Limitations}
With only $n=5$ datasets, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test is mathematically underpowered, as the $p$-value cannot fall below $0.0625$ regardless of effect size. This motivates our cohort-wide expansion to 20 datasets using the Friedman/Nemenyi framework, which establishes robust statistical comparisons without this limitation.

\subsection{Architectural Trade-Offs, Interpretability, and Practical Utility}
Selecting an appropriate time series classification architecture requires balancing classification accuracy against computational speed, memory footprint, noise resilience, and model explainability. Contemporary state-of-the-art architectures establish their benchmark accuracy through heavy ensemble models such as HIVE-COTE 2.0 \cite{middlehurst2021}, deep convolutional networks like InceptionTime \cite{fawaz2020}, or massive random convolutional projections such as ROCKET and MiniROCKET \cite{dempster2021}. While these models yield superior accuracy on static single splits, they introduce severe practical limitations: HIVE-COTE requires prohibitive multi-hour training budgets and massive memory footprints, InceptionTime functions as an opaque deep neural network requiring extensive gradient backpropagation, and ROCKET maps signals into 10,000 to 50,000 uninterpretable random features where individual predictive drivers cannot be physically inspected.

Our Proposed Adaptive LFIG framework offers a fundamentally distinct value proposition tailored to resource-constrained, real-time, and safety-critical environments. First, by compressing raw sequences of length $N$ into $S \ll N$ fuzzy granules, dynamic programming operates in $O(S^2)$ rather than $O(N^2)$, unlocking execution speedups of up to 14.2$\times$ over standard DTW and reducing peak heap memory allocations by up to 5.5$\times$. This makes our pipeline readily deployable on edge devices, low-power industrial microcontrollers, and real-time battery-powered medical monitors. Second, unlike black-box deep embeddings, every component of our 10D granular representation corresponds to a verified physical property (such as trend slope, Shannon entropy, volatility, curvature, and energy), allowing clinicians and engineers to visually audit anomaly boundaries and explain decision rationales directly from the fuzzy envelope $[L, U]$. For instance, in wearable ECG monitoring, a clinician can directly verify whether a classification was triggered by localized slope reversal, energy spikes, or Shannon entropy collapse during cardiac arrhythmia, satisfying strict regulatory explainability requirements. Similarly, in industrial turbine telemetry, real-time CPD detects structural vibration regime shifts instantly while entropy descriptors track mechanical wear without requiring multi-gigabyte neural network deployments. Third, the integration of least-squares trend estimation acts as an analytical filter that confers native resilience against high-frequency sensor noise without discarding measurement uncertainty. Consequently, whenever transparent decision-making, low latency, minimal memory overhead, and interpretable physical explanations are required, our proposed architecture provides a principled and computationally efficient alternative to heavy black-box baselines.

\section{Conclusion}
We have presented an Adaptive Multi-Feature LFIG framework for time series classification. By dynamically routing signal segmentation, extracting 10-dimensional statistical-structural feature spaces, and fusing Hausdorff-DTW-Cosine distances, the framework achieves statistical parity with DTW-1NN under Nemenyi critical-difference testing while running up to 14.2$\times$ faster and reducing peak heap memory by up to 5.5$\times$. While state-of-the-art ensemble models (such as HIVE-COTE 2.0) and convolutional models (such as ROCKET) lead on raw single-split accuracies due to high parameter capacity, our framework offers an efficient, physically explainable alternative for resource-constrained architectures. It provides a massive reduction in computational footprint and memory usage, while offering human-interpretable explanations of local trend dynamics and fuzzy boundary envelopes.

\subsection{Future Work}
Future investigations will advance the proposed framework along three natural research trajectories. First, we plan to generalize the 10-dimensional granular representation and hybrid similarity metrics to multivariate time series benchmarks, developing inter-channel co-granulation mechanisms for multi-lead physiological and kinematic sensor streams. Second, to accelerate pairwise distance evaluations on massive datasets, we intend to implement native CUDA-parallel dynamic programming kernels in PyTorch or JAX to execute alignments concurrently on GPU warps. Third, we aim to integrate reference prototype editing algorithms (such as $k$-medoids or condensed nearest neighbors) to prune redundant training references and further minimize inference latency for real-time edge deployments.
