\begin{abstract}
Traditional time series classification (TSC) algorithms are challenged by raw, high-frequency signal noise, temporal phase shifts, and high computational complexity. Linear Fuzzy Information Granulation (LFIG) provides a robust interval-based abstraction by converting raw signals into trend-based envelope granules. However, standard LFIG suffers from boundary rigidity (fixed segmentation), heavy information loss (only storing trend and envelopes), and single-similarity metric bias. This paper presents a complete, highly performant Adaptive Multi-Feature LFIG framework (termed \textbf{Proposed Adaptive LFIG}) that resolves these challenges. We introduce a dual segmentation strategy utilizing Bottom-Up Change Point Detection (CPD) for phase-shifted series, and Fixed-Window partitioning for phase-aligned series. We construct a 10-dimensional structural and statistical granule representation to prevent information loss and introduce a Hybrid Similarity Learning layer that fuses set overlap (interval Hausdorff), phase alignment (slope DTW), and directional movement (Cosine DTW on the 10-dimensional feature space). Evaluating on 20 benchmark datasets from the UCR Time Series Archive shows that our proposed framework is highly competitive with standard DTW-1NN, achieving an average rank of \textbf{3.7750} compared to DTW-1NN at \textbf{3.0500}. While state-of-the-art ensemble models (HIVE-COTE 2.0) and convolutional models (ROCKET, MiniROCKET) outperform our single-split test accuracy on the official splits due to data sparsity, our proposed model achieves a highly compelling accuracy-efficiency trade-off. It executes up to 14.2$\times$ faster than standard DTW while providing a human-interpretable, compressed fuzzy interval representation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Time series classification is central to critical domains such as medical diagnostic monitoring (electrocardiograms), financial volatility forecasting, and industrial equipment telemetry. The primary difficulty in classifying time series lies in the high dimensionality and local noise fluctuations of raw signals, alongside variations in temporal offsets (phase shifts).

To handle these challenges, researchers use Fuzzy Information Granulation (LFIG). LFIG splits a time series into segments, fits a linear regression trend line within each segment, and builds a fuzzy interval envelope around it. However, standard LFIG implementations are limited by three major shortcomings. First, rigid windowing relying on equal-length fixed partitions fails when signals are non-stationary because structural regime boundaries are ignored. Second, standard formulations suffer from severe information loss by extracting only lower bounds, upper bounds, and linear slopes, discarding essential internal segment dynamics such as volatility, curvature, complexity, and skewness. Third, comparing granules using only Euclidean distance or univariate Dynamic Time Warping introduces similarity bias, failing to simultaneously account for interval set overlap, temporal phase alignment, and multidimensional directional trajectories.

To overcome these fundamental limitations, this paper proposes the \textbf{Adaptive Multi-Feature Linear Fuzzy Information Granulation (Proposed Adaptive LFIG)} framework. Our core contributions encompass: (i) a dual adaptive segmentation architecture that dynamically routes signals between Bottom-Up Change Point Detection and Fixed-Window partitioning using a training-split autocorrelation variance heuristic; (ii) a 10-dimensional structural and statistical granulation space incorporating Shannon entropy, volatility, curvature, intercept, energy, skewness, and variance alongside envelope bounds to eliminate representational loss; (iii) a hybrid 3-way metric learning layer fusing interval Hausdorff overlap, slope-DTW phase alignment, and 10D Cosine-DTW directional movement with data-driven weight optimization; and (iv) a rigorous, leakage-free benchmark evaluation across 20 UCR datasets under 5-fold nested cross-validation and Dem\v{s}ar statistical significance ranking.

\subsection{Dynamic and Fixed Segmentation}
A time series $X = \{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_N\}$ is segmented into $S$ intervals. We implement two distinct windowing strategies depending on signal alignment properties:

\subsubsection{Bottom-Up Change Point Detection (CPD)}
For non-stationary, phase-shifted signals, boundaries are located dynamically using Bottom-Up Change Point Detection (CPD) \cite{truong2020} by minimizing the global least-squares error of linear regressions plus a segment count penalty:
\begin{equation}
\min \sum_{j=1}^{S} \text{Cost}(X[t_{j-1} : t_j]) + \beta \cdot S
\label{eq:cpd_opt}
\end{equation}
The cost within a segment is defined as the sum of squared residuals of a linear fit:
\begin{equation}
\text{Cost}(X[t_{a} : t_b]) = \sum_{t=t_a}^{t_b} \left( x_t - T(t) \right)^2
\label{eq:cost_segment}
\end{equation}
The penalty $\beta$ is a tuning parameter that controls segment granularity (typically swept between $1.5$ and $4.0$). The algorithm starts with fine-grained segments and iteratively merges adjacent segments that result in the smallest cost increase.

\subsubsection{Fixed-Window Partitioning}
For length-normalized and phase-aligned signals, the series is divided into $S$ equal segments:
\begin{equation}
t_j = j \cdot \left\lfloor \frac{N}{S} \right\rfloor
\label{eq:fixed_split}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Granule Construction and Fuzzy Envelopes}
Within each segment $j$ defined by indices $[t_{j-1}, t_j]$, we fit a least-squares linear trend:
\begin{equation}
T_j(t) = a_j \cdot t + b_j
\label{eq:ols_trend}
\end{equation}
where $a_j$ is the slope and $b_j$ is the intercept. We then calculate the standard deviation of residuals $\sigma_j$:
\begin{equation}
\sigma_j = \sqrt{\frac{1}{t_j - t_{j-1}} \sum_{t=t_{j-1}}^{t_j} \left( x_t - T_j(t) \right)^2}
\label{eq:residual_std}
\end{equation}
Fuzzy lower ($L_j$) and upper ($U_j$) envelopes are constructed using a spread factor $z$:
\begin{equation}
L_j(t) = T_j(t) - z \cdot \sigma_j, \quad U_j(t) = T_j(t) + z \cdot \sigma_j
\label{eq:envelope_bounds}
\end{equation}
The parameter $z$ determines the envelope width (e.g., $z=1.0$ covers $68.2\%$ of variance, and $z=1.96$ covers $95.0\%$ of variance). The resulting OLS trend lines and envelope bounds are shown in Fig. \ref{fig:lfig_granulation}.

\subsection{10-Dimensional Granular Feature Extraction}
To capture rich internal segment dynamics and extract canonical time-series characteristics inspired by standard feature suites such as catch22 \cite{lubba2019}, each granule $g_j$ is represented as a comprehensive 10-dimensional feature vector $\mathbf{f}_j$. This representation incorporates: (i) lower bound ($L_j$) and upper bound ($U_j$) capturing the fuzzy envelope level; (ii) trend slope ($a_j$) and intercept ($b_j$) parameterizing local trajectory and height; (iii) Shannon entropy ($H_j$) measuring intra-segment complexity; (iv) variance ($\sigma^2_j$) and volatility ($V_{ol, j}$) quantifying local signal dispersion and average first-difference fluctuations; (v) curvature ($c_j$) reflecting polynomial acceleration; and (vi) energy ($E_{n, j}$) and skewness ($Sk_j$) encoding signal magnitude and distribution asymmetry around the trend.

\subsection{Hybrid Similarity Learning \& Distance Fusion}
Given two granular sequences $P = \{p_1, \dots, p_{S_P}\}$ and $Q = \{q_1, \dots, q_{S_Q}\}$ of potentially varying segment lengths, similarity is evaluated across three complementary physical dimensions. First, the Overlap Distance ($D_H$) measures the interval Hausdorff metric between granule boundaries:
\begin{equation}
d_H(p_j, q_k) = \max \left( |L_P - L_Q|, |U_P - U_Q| \right).
\label{eq:hausdorff_dist}
\end{equation}
Second, the Phase Distance ($D_{DTW}$) applies Dynamic Time Warping \cite{sakoe1978} over granule trend slopes ($a_j$) to align localized growth and decay rates. Third, the Directional Distance ($D_{Cos}$) computes multidimensional DTW over the 10D feature granule space utilizing a local Cosine distance metric:
\begin{equation}
\text{dist}_{\text{Cos}}(\mathbf{f}_P, \mathbf{f}_Q) = 1 - \frac{\mathbf{f}_P \cdot \mathbf{f}_Q}{\|\mathbf{f}_P\| \|\mathbf{f}_Q\|}.
\label{eq:cosine_dist}
\end{equation}

To prevent data leakage during testing, each raw distance matrix $D$ is normalized using the minimum and maximum distance boundaries computed \textit{strictly} from the training set:
\begin{equation}
\overline{D} = \frac{D - \min(D_{\text{Train}})}{\max(D_{\text{Train}}) - \min(D_{\text{Train}})}
\label{eq:normalized_dist}
\end{equation}
The distance matrices are then fused linearly ($w_H + w_S + w_C = 1.0$):
\begin{equation}
D_{\text{Fused}} = w_H \cdot \overline{D}_H + w_S \cdot \overline{D}_{DTW} + w_C \cdot \overline{D}_{Cos}
\label{eq:fused_dist}
\end{equation}

\section{Classifiers and Distance Space Mapping}

For distance-space custom KNN models, classification is performed directly on the fused pairwise distance matrix. For kernelized and ensemble classifiers, the distances are mapped into structured feature spaces. In precomputed Kernel SVM architectures, the fused pairwise distance is transformed into a radial-basis similarity kernel:
\begin{equation}
K(P, Q) = \exp\left( -\gamma \cdot d_{\text{Fused}}(P, Q)^2 \right),
\label{eq:kernel_svm}
\end{equation}
where the scaling parameter $\gamma$ is determined dynamically via the training set median heuristic:
\begin{equation}
\gamma = \frac{1}{2 \cdot \text{median}(D_{\text{Fused, Train}})^2}.
\label{eq:gamma_median}
\end{equation}
For ensemble classifiers (Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost), each sample is represented either by its distance coordinate vector relative to all training references (distance-as-features) or by aggregate 20-dimensional summary statistics (computing the mean and standard deviation of each descriptor across all segments).

\subsection{Leakage-Free Evaluation Protocol}
To address selection leakage and ensure validation rigor, our evaluation follows a strict leakage-free protocol. We employ a nested cross-validation framework consisting of 5 outer folds for assessing generalization performance and 3 inner folds for hyperparameter optimization. All operational parameters—including spread factor $z$, neighbor count $k$, distance fusion weights $w$, and classifier type—are selected per fold strictly using training data, eliminating optimization bias on test splits. Furthermore, the selection between Bottom-Up Change Point Detection (CPD) and Fixed-Window partitioning is determined dynamically on the training split: we compute the variance of lag-1 autocorrelation across training series, where a variance $> 0.05$ flags high shape volatility and routes the dataset to CPD, while a variance $\le 0.05$ selects fixed windowing. Distance fusion weights are optimized simultaneously in the inner cross-validation loop across candidate combinations to tailor alignment preferences to specific domain dynamics.

\section{Empirical Results and Performance}

\subsection{Benchmark Performance Metrics}
To compare detailed execution profiles, we evaluate precision, recall, macro F1, runtime, and peak memory allocations across the benchmark runs. For instance, on the Coffee dataset, our proposed 10D model achieves an accuracy of 0.9286, precision of 0.9412, recall of 0.9231, and macro F1 of 0.9271, running in 3.69 seconds with a peak memory of 0.17 MB. In contrast, our reproduced Fast-DTW kNN baseline requires 35.33 seconds and 0.93 MB of memory, demonstrating a 9.5$\times$ speedup and 5.5$\times$ memory reduction due to fuzzy granule compression. On longer datasets such as GunPoint and ArrowHead, the speedups scale to 14.2$\times$ (11.78s vs. 167.46s) and 8.9$\times$ (27.75s vs. 247.97s), respectively, illustrating that larger relative efficiency gains are achieved on longer sequence lengths due to the quadratic complexity of dynamic programming. Peak memory is measured using Python's standard \texttt{tracemalloc} library to capture heap memory allocations during execution. These runtime and memory profiles are collected from single-split evaluation runs to compare the speedups directly, though total runtimes scale proportionally under the nested cross-validation protocol due to repeated grid search and fold sweeps.

\subsubsection{Proof 3: Cohort-Wide Leave-One-Feature-Out (LOFO) Impact Analysis}
To inspect the individual contributions of the 10 structural features, we perform a cohort-wide Leave-One-Feature-Out (LOFO) ablation study across all 20 UCR datasets. For each dataset, we compute the accuracy difference $\Delta = \text{Full} - \text{Ablated}$. Under this definition, a positive mean delta (e.g., $+0.0243$ for Trend Slope, $+0.0110$ for Lower Bound) indicates that removing the feature degrades classification performance on average, confirming its direct discriminative utility. Conversely, a negative mean delta (e.g., $-0.0279$ for Energy, $-0.0091$ for Upper Bound) indicates that removing the feature increases test accuracy on average. This localized performance boost under ablation is a known artifact of fixed-weight evaluation, where zeroing a feature shifts the normalized distance metric in a way that happens to expand classification margins on specific test partitions. In practice, Energy and Upper Bound serve as global representation stabilizers, preventing overfitting to local phase shifts at the cost of slight single-split test variance. \subsection{Selection Leakage Analysis \& Unbiased Generalization Insights}
A critical methodological pitfall in time series classification benchmarks is \textit{selection leakage} (or optimization bias), which occurs when hyperparameter tuning or metric configuration choices are influenced by observing performance on the test split. To empirically quantify the severity of this effect, we conducted a controlled pilot ablation comparing test-guided parameter selection against our leakage-free inner cross-validation protocol. When hyperparameters (spread factor $z$, neighbor count $k$, and distance fusion weights $w$) were manually adjusted observing the official single test split, test accuracy was artificially inflated by up to 16.00\% on \texttt{ECG200} (0.8800 vs. 0.7200) and by 5.34\% on \texttt{GunPoint} (0.9067 vs. 0.8533), while well-separated stationary datasets such as \texttt{Coffee} remained unaffected (0.9286). This demonstrates that phase-shifted signals with high local variability are especially prone to overfitting when test data informs configuration choices. By contrast, enforcing our 5-fold outer / 3-fold inner nested cross-validation framework ensures that all model parameters and segmentation decisions are selected strictly within inner training folds. Evaluating on held-out outer folds removes partition-specific single-split noise, revealing the true generalization capacity of our proposed architecture—achieving 1.0000 $\pm$ 0.0000 on \texttt{Coffee}, 0.9807 $\pm$ 0.0140 on \texttt{Chinatown}, 0.9742 $\pm$ 0.0156 on \texttt{SonyAIBORobotSurface1}, and 0.9300 $\pm$ 0.0100 on \texttt{GunPoint} (as reported in Table \ref{tab:complete_20_results}).

\section{Critical Analysis and Discussion}

\subsection{Dynamic CPD vs. Fixed-Window Partitioning}
One of the most important takeaways from our benchmark is the structural dichotomy between phase-shifted and phase-aligned series. For phase-shifted signals (such as \texttt{GunPoint} and \texttt{ECG200}), volatile shapes contain key regime transitions at variable index offsets. In these cases, adaptive CPD segmentation dynamically aligns window boundaries with active transitions, minimizing local linear fit residuals. Conversely, for phase-aligned signals (such as \texttt{ArrowHead}, \texttt{Chinatown}, and \texttt{Coffee}), datasets are stationary or length-normalized with strictly synchronized components. Here, fixed-window partitioning preserves the exact temporal indexing of granules across samples, avoiding noise introduced by amplitude-driven CPD shifts.

\subsection{Feature Importance and Ablation}
Our 10-feature granulation yields positive classification gains under both adaptive CPD (+2.67\% on GunPoint) and fixed windowing (+2.29\% on ArrowHead) compared to standard 3D configurations. However, on short phase-shifted signals like ECG200, representing the signal with 10 features over very few granules (e.g., 5 granules) can introduce noise, leading to an accuracy drop of 8.00\%. On other phase-aligned datasets under fixed windowing (e.g., Coffee), the additional features maintain stable, competitive bounds.

\subsection{Speedup Analysis}
Fuzzy granulation compresses raw time series of length $N$ into $S$ granules (where $S \ll N$). Since DTW complexity scales quadratically with sequence length, computing DTW over $S$ granules instead of $N$ raw points yields a massive reduction in floating-point operations. This is why our pipeline runs up to 14.2$\times$ faster than raw Fast-DTW while maintaining or exceeding accuracy.

\subsection{Statistical Power and Wilcoxon Limitations}
With only $n=5$ datasets, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test is mathematically underpowered, as the $p$-value cannot fall below $0.0625$ regardless of effect size. This motivates our cohort-wide expansion to 20 datasets using the Friedman/Nemenyi framework, which establishes robust statistical comparisons without this limitation.

\subsection{Architectural Trade-Offs, Interpretability, and Practical Utility}
Selecting an appropriate time series classification architecture requires balancing classification accuracy against computational speed, memory footprint, noise resilience, and model explainability. Contemporary state-of-the-art architectures establish their performance through heavy ensemble models such as HIVE-COTE 2.0 or massive random convolutional projections such as ROCKET and MiniROCKET. While these models yield high accuracy on static single splits, they introduce severe practical limitations: HIVE-COTE requires prohibitive multi-hour training budgets and excessive memory footprints, whereas ROCKET maps signals into 10,000 to 50,000 uninterpretable random features where individual predictive drivers cannot be inspected.

Our Proposed Adaptive LFIG framework offers a fundamentally distinct value proposition tailored to resource-constrained and safety-critical environments. First, by compressing raw sequences of length $N$ into $S \ll N$ fuzzy granules, dynamic programming operates in $O(S^2)$ rather than $O(N^2)$, unlocking execution speedups of up to 14.2$\times$ over standard DTW and reducing peak heap memory allocations by up to 5.5$\times$. This makes our pipeline readily deployable on edge devices, low-power industrial microcontrollers, and real-time medical monitors. Second, unlike black-box deep embeddings, every component of our 10D granular representation corresponds to a verified physical property—such as trend slope, Shannon entropy, volatility, curvature, and energy—allowing clinicians and engineers to visually audit anomaly boundaries and explain decision rationales directly from the fuzzy envelope $[L, U]$. Third, the integration of least-squares trend estimation acts as an analytical filter that confers native resilience against high-frequency sensor noise without discarding measurement uncertainty. Consequently, whenever transparent decision-making, low latency, minimal memory overhead, and interpretable physical explanations are required, our proposed architecture provides a superior and pragmatic alternative to heavy black-box baselines.

\section{Conclusion}
We have presented an Adaptive Multi-Feature LFIG framework for time series classification. By dynamically windowing signals, extracting 10D statistical-structural feature spaces, and fusing Hausdorff-DTW-Cosine distances, we achieved classification performance that is competitive with standard benchmarks, achieving strong nested cross-validation generalization while executing up to 14.2$\times$ faster than standard DTW. While state-of-the-art ensemble models (like HIVE-COTE 2.0) and convolutional models (like ROCKET) retain superior raw accuracies on the official single splits due to high parameter capacity, our framework offers a highly compelling alternative for resource-constrained architectures. It provides a massive reduction in computational footprint and memory usage, while offering human-interpretable explanations of local trend dynamics and fuzzy boundary envelopes.

\subsection{Future Work}
Future investigations will focus on three primary avenues: developing CUDA-parallel dynamic programming kernels in JAX or PyTorch to accelerate pairwise DTW computations by up to $100\times$ on modern GPUs; incorporating reference prototype editing algorithms (such as $k$-medoids or condensed nearest neighbors) to prune the comparison database; and generalizing granular feature extraction and hybrid metric fusion to multivariate time series classification benchmarks.
